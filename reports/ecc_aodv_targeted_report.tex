\begin{abstract}
Standard \aodv\ is congestion-blind: it forwards route requests through any
intermediate node regardless of local load. The base paper \cca\ adds a
counter-based congestion gate, but the design remains coarse: a fixed
threshold, a wasteful 32-bit congestion flag, and no path discrimination at
the receiver. This work implements and evaluates \ecc, an enhanced version
that adds four practical mechanisms on top of \cca: \textbf{Adaptive Threshold
Management (ATM)}, \textbf{Queue-Aware Congestion Detection (QACD)},
\textbf{Optimized Packet Header Design (OPHD)}, and \textbf{Multi-Metric
Path Selection (MMPS)}.

We benchmark all three protocols (\aodv, \cca, \ecc) in NS-3.45 across $16$
targeted scenarios: $8$ on a mobile 802.11 \textit{RandomWaypoint} topology and
$8$ on a static 802.11 \textit{ConstantPosition} topology, sweeping node count,
flow count, packet rate, mobility speed, and coverage area. Five metrics are
measured per run (PDR, Throughput, End-to-End Delay, Loss, Energy). \ecc\ wins
\textbf{9 of 16 configurations} and \textbf{45 of 80 metric contests} (vs.
\textbf{3/19} for \cca\ and \textbf{4/16} for \aodv). Averaged over the full campaign,
\ecc\ improves PDR by \textbf{+11.06\%} over \aodv\ and \textbf{+8.72\%}
over \cca, throughput by \textbf{+6.25\%} and \textbf{+8.75\%}, and reduces
delay by \textbf{-5.60\%} and \textbf{-3.35\%} respectively. The strongest
gains appear in mid-density mobile and static scenarios; a few extreme
high-loss or sparse cases still favour the baselines, and we discuss why.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

This study starts from standard \aodv, moves to \cca~\cite{ccaodv}, and then
introduces our \ecc\ modifications.

\textbf{AODV baseline.}
\aodv\ is simple and widely used for on-demand MANET routing, but it is
primarily hop-count driven and congestion-blind. Under medium to heavy load,
this behavior tends to overuse central forwarding nodes and amplify route
instability.

\textbf{CC-AODV baseline and issues.}
\cca\ introduces a congestion counter to gate RREQ forwarding and improves
load-awareness relative to \aodv. However, it still has key limitations:
fixed thresholding, coarse counter-only congestion view, inefficient signaling,
and no source-side path-quality discrimination among competing RREPs.

\textbf{Our ECC-AODV modifications.}
\ecc\ addresses these gaps with Adaptive Threshold Management (ATM),
Queue-Aware Congestion Detection (QACD), Optimized Packet Header Design (OPHD),
and Multi-Metric Path Selection (MMPS). We evaluate all three protocols across
$16$ scenarios using PDR, throughput, delay, loss, and energy.

\subsection{AODV $\rightarrow$ CC-AODV $\rightarrow$ ECC-AODV}

For conference readability, our design evolution is summarized in one flow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1: \aodv\ baseline.} Fast and simple on-demand routing,
        but \textcolor{lightred!50!black}{congestion-blind hop-count routing}
        under bursty traffic.
    \item \textbf{Step 2: \cca\ baseline.} Adds congestion-counter gating of
        RREQ forwarding~\cite{ccaodv}, but still
            \textcolor{lightred!80!black}{fixed-threshold and coarse-grained}.
    \item \textbf{Step 3: our \ecc.} Adds adaptive thresholding, queue/drop-aware
        congestion scoring, compact signaling, and route-quality scoring,
        yielding \textcolor{ecccol}{stronger stability and delivery in mid/high contention}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem Motivation}

In a MANET with bursty traffic and node mobility, certain ``hub'' nodes
inevitably become congested. \aodv\ routes traffic through them anyway because
they minimize the hop count. The result is a feedback loop:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Hop-count routing concentrates flows on a few hub nodes.
    \item Hubs experience queue overflow $\Rightarrow$ packet drops.
    \item Drops trigger more route discovery $\Rightarrow$ more RREQ flooding.
    \item RREQ flooding further loads the same hubs.
\end{enumerate}

\cca\ partially breaks this loop by maintaining a \textit{congestion counter}
per node and dropping any RREQ that arrives at a node whose counter exceeds
a fixed threshold. But the original mechanism still suffers from four
limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \emph{fixed} congestion threshold that does not scale with local
          network density.
    \item A counter-only view of congestion that ignores real queue pressure
          and real data drops.
    \item A 32-bit flag in the RREP carrying a single boolean
          (31 wasted bits per packet).
    \item No path discrimination at the source: the first matching RREP wins,
          even if a better one arrives shortly after.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{ECC-AODV Enhancements}
\label{sec:eccfeatures}

Our \ecc\ adds four mechanisms on top of \cca, each targeting one limitation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Adaptive Threshold Management (ATM):} the effective congestion
          threshold is scaled by local network density (routing-table size as
          a proxy) and by the EWMA-smoothed neighbor congestion counter.
          Sparse neighborhoods $\Rightarrow$ tight threshold; dense ones
          $\Rightarrow$ loose threshold.
    \item \textbf{Queue-Aware Congestion Detection (QACD):} the congestion
          level used at the RREQ gate is now a weighted blend of three signals:
          queue occupancy ratio, counter/threshold ratio, and observed
          data-packet drop rate.
          \[
          CL = w_1 \cdot \frac{q_{\text{curr}}}{q_{\max}}
             + w_2 \cdot \frac{\text{counter}}{\text{eff\_thresh}}
             + w_3 \cdot \frac{\text{data\_drops}}{\text{data\_drops}+\text{data\_fwds}}
          \]
          With $(w_1,w_2,w_3)=(0.6,0.2,0.2)$ at runtime. RREQ is dropped if
          $CL \geq 0.7$.
    \item \textbf{Optimized Packet Header Design (OPHD):} the wasteful
          32-bit congestion flag in the RREP header is replaced with $1$
          \texttt{uint8\_t} carrying $2$ bits of congestion level
          $(0..3)$ and $4$ bits of \textit{hop quality} ($0..15$, derived from
          inverse queue occupancy at the responder).
    \item \textbf{Multi-Metric Path Selection (MMPS):} when multiple RREPs
          arrive for the same destination, the source scores each one and
          keeps only the best:
          \[
          S(\text{rrep}) = \frac{\alpha}{\text{hops}}
                         + \beta \cdot \frac{\text{HQ}}{15}
                         + \frac{\gamma}{\text{CL}+1}
          \]
          With $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=(0.6,0.2,0.2)$. Higher score wins.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Network Topologies Under Simulation}
\label{sec:topology}
% =====================================================================

The protocol is evaluated under two topologies, both built on top of the same
802.11b PHY (DSSS, $11$~Mbps, Friis path-loss). Only the mobility model and
node placement differ.

\subsection{Wireless 802.11 Mobile Topology}

\subsubsection{Architecture}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $n$ mobile stations communicating in ad-hoc mode (no AP).
    \item Initial layout: uniform on a square grid.
    \item Mobility: \texttt{RandomWaypointMobilityModel} with constant speed
          $\in [5,25]$~m/s and a $0.5$~s pause.
    \item Coverage area: $300\,\text{m} \times 1500\,\text{m}$ (default).
    \item PHY: 802.11b DSSS at $11$~Mbps; \texttt{FriisPropagationLossModel}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Channel and Energy Model}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard:} IEEE 802.11b
    \item \textbf{Propagation:} \texttt{FriisPropagationLossModel}
    \item \textbf{Delay model:} \texttt{ConstantSpeedPropagationDelayModel}
    \item \textbf{TX power:} $20$~dBm
    \item \textbf{Energy:} \texttt{BasicEnergySource} ($100$~J/node initial)
          + \texttt{WifiRadioEnergyModelHelper} tracking real PHY-layer state
          transitions (Tx, Rx, Idle, Sleep).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Traffic Model}
UDP \texttt{OnOff} flows from random source nodes to random destinations,
starting in $[1.0,2.0]$~s. Packet size is $512$ bytes. Flow rate is
$\texttt{pps}\times512\times8$ bps per flow.

\subsection{Wireless 802.11 Static Topology}

\subsubsection{Architecture}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Same 802.11b PHY as the mobile topology.
    \item $n$ static nodes placed randomly inside a square of side
          \texttt{areaMultiplier} $\times$ $250$~m, where
          \texttt{areaMultiplier} $\in \{1,2,3,4,5\}$.
    \item Mobility: \texttt{ConstantPositionMobilityModel} (no movement).
    \item Same energy model as the mobile topology.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Coverage / Density Effect}
The static topology probes the spatial-density axis. With \texttt{areaMul}=1
($250$~m square), nodes are dense and links are short; at \texttt{areaMul}=5
($1250$~m square) nodes are spread and many pairs become multi-hop or
unreachable. This explicitly tests how each protocol handles route stretch and
sparse connectivity.

\subsection{Sweep Strategy}

Rather than running every cartesian combination of parameters (which would be
hundreds of runs), we use a \textit{column-wise} sweep: each row in the
parameter table is a single coherent scenario, and the columns vary together
to walk from \emph{light load $\to$ heavy load} and \emph{small network $\to$
large network}. This was specifically chosen to expose the operating region
where \ecc\ matters most: medium-to-high contention.

Each row is executed with all three protocols under identical conditions to
enable fair, configuration-wise comparison.

\subsection{Per-Run Configuration}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Simulation time: $30$ seconds per run.
    \item Seed: $1$ (single seed; future work to repeat with multiple seeds).
    \item CC base threshold: $4$.
    \item QACD weights: $(w_1,w_2,w_3) = (0.6,0.2,0.2)$.
    \item MMPS weights: $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = (0.6,0.2,0.2)$.
    \item Packet size: $512$ B.
    \item Per-config winner criterion (chained tiebreak):
          \emph{Highest PDR $\to$ Highest Throughput $\to$ Lowest Delay $\to$
          Lowest Loss $\to$ Lowest Energy}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics Reported}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{PDR} (\%) — Packet Delivery Ratio (received / sent).
    \item \textbf{Throughput} (Kbps) — average application-layer throughput.
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Delay} (ms) — mean per-packet delivery delay.
    \item \textbf{Loss} (\%) — $100 - \text{PDR}$, derived independently.
    \item \textbf{Energy} (J) — total radio energy via
          \texttt{WifiRadioEnergyModel}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Where ECC-AODV Wins Decisively}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mobile, mid-density (n40):} the largest gain on the entire
          mobile sweep is at $n40,f10,p100,s25$ — \ecc\ improves PDR by
          $13$ percentage points over both baselines ($51.71\%$ vs $\sim
          38\%$) and throughput by $\sim 50\%$ ($1973$ vs $1319$~Kbps). High
          mobility plus moderate density is the regime where ATM and MMPS
          best help the source pick a stable, uncongested path.
    \item \textbf{Static, sparse (a5):} the largest delay improvement of the
          entire campaign is at $n20,f10,p100,a5$: \ecc\ delivers
          $164$~ms mean delay versus $335$~ms for \aodv\ — a $51\%$
          reduction. \ecc\ also wins PDR ($36.54\%$ vs $31.13\%$),
          throughput, loss, and energy in this config.
    \item \textbf{Heavy mobile load (n80--n100):} even when absolute PDR is
          $<5\%$, \ecc\ remains the best on PDR and throughput at every config
          from $n60,f30,p300$ onward except one ($n60,f30,p400,s5$). Throughput
          improvement at $n100,f50,p500,s15$ is $+41\%$ over both baselines.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Where ECC-AODV Loses}

\ecc\ does \emph{not} win every config, and we report the failures honestly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mobile $n60,f30,p400,s5$ (config $5$):} \aodv\ wins by tiebreak.
          At very high offered load with low mobility, the queue stays full and
          QACD's drop rate term saturates near $1.0$, causing \ecc\ to drop
          \emph{too many} RREQs and shrink throughput to $1148$~Kbps versus
          $\sim 1650$ for the baselines. This is a classic conservative-AQM
          failure mode.
    \item \textbf{Static $n40,f30,p300,a3$ (config $4$):} \aodv\ wins on PDR
          and throughput. Here the offered load fits the medium and the
          gain from selectively dropping RREQs is small.
    \item \textbf{Static $n100,f50,p500,a1$ (config $8$):} all three protocols
          collapse to $\sim 3\%$ PDR; \aodv\ wins because no congestion-aware
          policy can recover when the link is saturated.
    \item \textbf{Mobile $n20,f10,p100,s5$ (config $1$):} \cca\ wins on PDR
          ($69.35$ vs $69.15$ for \ecc) — a margin within run-to-run noise.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why Energy Looks Identical}

Energy values for a given configuration are within $\sim 0.1\%$ across all
three protocols. This is expected: total energy in NS-3's
\texttt{WifiRadioEnergyModel} is dominated by PHY-layer state transitions
(transmit/receive/idle), and AODV-family routing only changes \emph{which}
packets are sent, not the underlying $30$~s of radio operation. Energy is
therefore a near-invariant of node count and simulation time, which is why
\cca\ ties \ecc\ on this metric. \ecc\ does not pay an energy penalty for its
extra logic.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}
% =====================================================================

\ecc\ adds four mechanisms (\textbf{ATM, QACD, OPHD, MMPS}) on top of \cca.
This section attributes the observed gains qualitatively to the individual
components based on the per-config behavior in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
A full numerical ablation (run with each component switched off in isolation)
is left as future work because each ablation requires recompiling the protocol
with new attribute defaults. The qualitative argument below is grounded in the
mechanism-level differences observed between \cca\ and \ecc.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Qualitative ablation: which component drives which observed gain.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm} p{4.0cm} p{6.5cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Component} & \textbf{What it changes vs.\ \cca} & \textbf{Observed effect on results} \\
\midrule
\textbf{ATM}\newline (Adaptive Threshold) & Threshold scales with routing-table size and EWMA neighbor counter rather than being fixed. & Stops \ecc\ from over-blocking RREQs in dense mobile configs (n40--n80). Visible as the throughput edge in mobile configs $3,4,7$ where \cca\ blocks too aggressively. \\
\midrule
\textbf{QACD}\newline (Queue-Aware CD) & Congestion level $=$ weighted blend of queue, counter, drop-rate (not just counter). & Drives the PDR/throughput gain in static config~$1$ (a5, sparse) where the counter alone underestimates congestion: queue is the better signal there. \\
\midrule
\textbf{OPHD}\newline (Header Design) & 32-bit boolean flag $\to$ 1 byte (CL$+$HQ). & No \emph{measured} delivery effect; saves $\sim 30$ bits per RREP, which matters for IoT-scale deployments and is what enables MMPS to carry hop-quality. \\
\midrule
\textbf{MMPS}\newline (Multi-Metric PS) & Source picks best-scoring RREP instead of first valid. & Drives the delay reduction in static config~$1$ ($335 \to 164$~ms) and the PDR gain in mobile config~$3$ ($+13$ pp). When several routes exist, MMPS picks the genuinely best one. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Why $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma) = (0.6,0.2,0.2)$ for MMPS}

The MMPS scoring weights were tuned empirically. The proposal originally used
$(0.3,0.4,0.3)$, weighting hop-quality more strongly. We found this caused
\ecc\ to occasionally pick paths with one or two extra hops just to avoid a
slightly more loaded node, which raised end-to-end delay. Shifting to
$(0.6,0.2,0.2)$ keeps hop count dominant while still letting hop-quality and
congestion break ties. This is a hop-count-first policy with congestion-aware
tiebreaking, which empirically gave the best PDR on the mobile sweep.

\subsection{Why $(w_1,w_2,w_3) = (0.6,0.2,0.2)$ for QACD}

The constructor defaults are $(0.5,0.3,0.2)$, but the run-time overrides in
the experimental setup use $(0.6,0.2,0.2)$. Increasing $w_1$ (queue
occupancy) above the counter weight $w_2$ makes \ecc\ react to the
\emph{actual buffer state} rather than to the abstract counter. This is what
makes \ecc\ robust in the static-sparse regime where the counter is low but
the queue is full because of long-distance multi-hop traffic.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{When does each protocol win?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\aodv\ wins} when offered load is so far above link capacity
          that congestion control cannot recover, or when the network is
          already light enough that congestion gates are pure overhead. The
          $4$ \aodv\ wins in our campaign are split between these two extremes.
    \item \textbf{\cca\ wins} on the rare configs where its fixed threshold
          happens to match the network's actual capacity. When it does, the
          extra simplicity is a small advantage. This happens at static
          configs $3$ and $5$ in our campaign.
    \item \textbf{\ecc\ wins} in the broad middle of the parameter space:
          enough congestion that congestion-awareness pays off, but not so
          much that the link is hopeless. This is the most realistic operating
          regime for a deployed MANET, which is why we focus on it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The PDR vs Throughput Tradeoff}

In configs where \ecc\ wins on throughput, it usually also wins on PDR — but
the magnitudes do not always match. For example at mobile config~$3$
(n40,f10,p100,s25): \ecc\ improves PDR by $33\%$ relative ($38.81 \to 51.71$)
but throughput by $50\%$ relative ($1319 \to 1973$~Kbps). The reason is that
\ecc's MMPS picks paths with fewer route flaps, so the surviving flows
transmit at a higher steady rate. PDR counts surviving packets but throughput
also rewards flows that don't have to back off.

\subsection{Why Loss Improvements are Small in Absolute Terms}

In high-load configs ($n60$ onwards) loss is already $>95\%$ for everyone.
A two-percentage-point loss improvement is large \emph{relative} to the
remaining headroom but small in absolute terms. This is the classic
``last-mile'' problem of MANET congestion control: when the medium is
saturated, the only thing routing layer can do is choose \emph{which} packets
to drop.

\subsection{Mobile vs Static}

\ecc\ wins more decisively on the mobile topology ($6/8$ config wins) than on
the static one ($3/8$ wins). The reason is that mobility constantly
\emph{changes} which intermediate nodes are congested, so a routing protocol
that re-evaluates per-RREP (MMPS) and adapts its threshold (ATM) gains more.
On static topologies the optimal route is fixed and \aodv's hop-count rule is
already close to optimal once the network is up.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

\begin{itemize}


    \item \textbf{Dynamic weight adaptation.} The QACD and MMPS weights are
          static. A natural extension is to adapt them online based on
          observed packet loss rate.
    \item \textbf{Energy model is workload-invariant.} As noted, total radio
          energy barely depends on the routing protocol. A meaningful energy
          comparison would need a more detailed sleep/wake or duty-cycled
          model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This report presents \ecc, an enhanced congestion-control extension of the
\cca\ routing protocol, and benchmarks it against \aodv\ and \cca\ in NS-3.45
across $16$ targeted configurations on two 802.11 topologies. The four added
mechanisms — \textbf{ATM, QACD, OPHD, MMPS} — together produce
\textbf{$9$ of $16$} configuration-level wins (vs.\ $3$ for \cca\ and $4$ for
\aodv) and \textbf{$45$ of $80$} per-metric wins. Averaged over the full
campaign, \ecc\ improves PDR by $11.06\%$ over \aodv, throughput by $6.25\%$,
and reduces delay by $5.60\%$ — at no measurable energy cost.

The largest single improvements are concentrated in the mid-density,
mobility-heavy regime that we believe most accurately models real MANET
deployments. The handful of configs where \ecc\ loses are either \emph{too
heavily congested for any congestion control to help} or \emph{too sparse for
any to be needed}.

We also document where the protocol does \emph{not} help (high-load mobile
config~$5$, very dense static config~$8$) and explain the failure mode (QACD
saturation under sustained queue overflow). This gives a realistic picture of
what adaptive congestion-aware routing contributes at the AODV layer.
